# English for a Technical Advisor

## Purpose

This guide is for a **Technical Advisor** who works with developers, product teams, project managers, business stakeholders, customers, and other technical teams.

The goal is to help you speak in a way that is:

- clear and professional;
- technical without sounding too complex;
- collaborative rather than controlling;
- suitable for international teams;
- easy to adapt for French and wider European work culture;
- useful in daily work, meetings, reviews, troubleshooting, and decision-making.

A Technical Advisor is not only someone who knows technology. The role is often to **understand the situation, ask the right questions, compare options, identify risks, guide the team, and help people make a good technical decision**.

---

# 1. How to describe your role

## Short version

> I work as a Technical Advisor. I help the team analyze technical topics, review solution options, identify risks, and make practical decisions.

## Natural version

> My role is to support the team on technical decisions. I usually help clarify the problem, review possible solutions, check constraints and risks, and make sure the proposed approach fits the wider system.

## More senior version

> I act as a technical point of reference for the team. I help connect business needs with technical reality, challenge assumptions when needed, and support teams in choosing solutions that are realistic, maintainable, and aligned with our standards.

## When speaking with non-technical people

> I help translate technical topics into clear options, risks, and next steps so that the team can make informed decisions.

## When speaking with developers

> I am here to support the technical direction, review options, help unblock difficult topics, and make sure we consider the impact beyond the immediate implementation.

# 2. How to answer the AI coding survey as a Technical Advisor

## Question 1
**How are you using AI coding tools in your day-to-day work right now?**

### Option A
> I use AI coding tools to analyze technical issues, review implementation approaches, troubleshoot problems, and understand unfamiliar code more quickly. I also use them to prepare technical notes, compare solution options, and support discussions with developers.

### Option B
> I mainly use AI as a technical assistant. It helps me explore possible solutions, validate my understanding, review code or logs, and prepare clearer explanations for the team.

### Option C
> I use AI coding tools to speed up technical analysis, investigate incidents, review design ideas, and prepare examples or documentation. I do not rely on the output directly; I always check it against the project context and technical constraints.

---

## Question 2
**How confident are you that AI coding tools are making you more productive?**

Recommended choice:

> **Confident**

Use **Very confident** only if AI already saves you a significant amount of time in your daily work.

---

## Question 3
**How would you describe your working relationship with AI coding tools?**

### Option A
> I use AI coding tools as a technical thought partner. They help me explore options and challenge assumptions, while I use my own experience and project context to validate the result.

### Option B
> I see AI as an assistant rather than a final decision-maker. It helps me work faster, but I still review the technical details, risks, and impact before recommending a solution.

### Option C
> I use AI to support my analysis and prepare ideas, but the final recommendation still depends on architecture, security, business needs, and the team’s experience.

---

## Question 4
**What’s getting in the way of you or your team using AI coding tools more effectively?**

### Option A
> The main challenge is giving AI enough business, technical, and architectural context. Without that context, the answer can be technically correct but not suitable for our environment.

### Option B
> Accuracy, data privacy, and project context are the main concerns. We also need clear ways to validate AI-generated suggestions before using them in real systems.

### Option C
> AI can be useful, but it does not always understand our internal constraints, legacy systems, or team standards. Better integration with project context would make it more effective.

---

## Question 5
**When AI-generated code isn’t right, what do you usually do first?**

Recommended answer:

> **Debug it together with the AI**

Alternative:

> **Reprompt with more detail**

Both fit a Technical Advisor role because they show that you first clarify the issue instead of accepting the result without review.

---

## Question 6
**What are you hoping to get out of this program?**

### Option A
> I would like to improve how I use AI for technical analysis, troubleshooting, solution evaluation, and developer collaboration. I also want to learn how to get more reliable results while using AI responsibly.

### Option B
> I want to learn better ways to use AI as part of technical decision-making, especially for investigation, architecture discussions, code review, and problem solving.

### Option C
> I am interested in practical methods for using AI in real technical work, while keeping good engineering judgment, security, and maintainability in mind.

# 3. Daily phrases for a Technical Advisor

## Starting a technical discussion

> Let me make sure I understand the issue correctly.

> Can we first agree on the problem we are trying to solve?

> Before we go into the solution, I would like to understand the current behavior.

> Could you walk me through the flow from start to finish?

> What is the main constraint here?

> Is this a technical limitation, a business rule, or both?

> What changed before the issue started?

> Do we know whether this is isolated or affecting several components?

> Let us separate the symptom from the root cause.

> I think we should first clarify the expected behavior.

---

## Asking for more context

> Could you give me a bit more context?

> Do we have an example from production?

> Can you share the logs or error message?

> Which service owns this part of the flow?

> Is this behavior documented somewhere?

> Has this happened before?

> What assumptions are we making here?

> Do we know the expected volume?

> Are there any security or compliance constraints?

> Is there a deadline that affects the solution?

---

# 4. Giving technical advice without sounding too strong

A Technical Advisor often needs to influence without sounding like they are giving orders.

## Direct but collaborative

Instead of:

> We have to do it this way.

Say:

> I would recommend this approach because it gives us a clearer separation of responsibilities.

Instead of:

> This solution is wrong.

Say:

> I see a few risks with this approach. Maybe we can review them before we decide.

Instead of:

> Do not use this.

Say:

> I would be careful with this option because it could create issues later.

Instead of:

> This will not work.

Say:

> I am not sure this will work well under the current constraints.

Instead of:

> You need to change the design.

Say:

> I think the design could benefit from one small adjustment.

# 5. Common meeting language

## Opening a meeting

> Thanks everyone for joining.

> The goal today is to agree on the technical direction.

> I suggest we focus on the main decision first and keep smaller details for later.

> I would like to make sure we leave the meeting with clear next steps.

> We have a few options to review today.

> Maybe we can start with the current situation and then discuss the proposed change.

---

## Structuring the discussion

> I see three points we need to cover.

> First, let us look at the current behavior.

> Second, we should review the main risks.

> Finally, we can agree on the preferred option.

> I suggest we separate short-term fixes from the long-term solution.

> Maybe we can look at this from both the application side and the infrastructure side.

> There are two different questions here.

> Let us take them one by one.

---

## Bringing the team back on topic

> I think this is useful, but maybe slightly outside today’s scope.

> Can we park this point and come back to the main question?

> I suggest we keep this as a follow-up item.

> Maybe we are going too deep into implementation details for now.

> For today, I think we only need to agree on the direction.

---

# 6. Showing your Technical Advisor role in meetings

These phrases help other people clearly see that you are guiding the technical discussion.

## Clarifying the problem

> Before recommending a solution, I would like to confirm the real source of the issue.

> From what I understand, the technical problem is not the API itself but the way the data is handled after the response.

> I think we have two different concerns here: reliability and performance.

> The current issue seems to be a combination of configuration and application behavior.

---

## Comparing options

> We have two realistic options.

> Option A is simpler and faster, but it gives us less flexibility later.

> Option B needs more work now, but it is easier to maintain in the long term.

> Technically, both options are possible. The main difference is cost, complexity, and operational impact.

> I would prefer the second option if we expect this area to grow.

> For a short-term fix, the first option is acceptable.

---

## Highlighting risks

> The main risk I see is around backward compatibility.

> We should also consider the operational impact.

> My concern is not the implementation itself, but how this behaves under higher load.

> This is technically possible, but it increases dependency between the two services.

> We should be careful not to introduce a temporary fix that becomes permanent.

> I would like us to check the security impact before we proceed.

> This could work, but we need a rollback plan.

---

## Making a recommendation

> Based on the current constraints, I would recommend option B.

> My preference would be to keep the change as small as possible.

> I think the safest approach is to validate this with a small proof of concept first.

> From a maintenance point of view, I would choose the simpler design.

> If there is no strong business reason to customize this, I would stay close to the standard solution.

---

# 7. How to disagree professionally

This is especially useful in French and European teams, where directness is often accepted, but it is better to stay factual and respectful.

## Soft disagreement

> I see your point, but I have a slightly different view.

> I understand the idea. My concern is mainly about the long-term impact.

> That could work. I am just not fully convinced about the operational side.

> I agree with the objective, but I am not sure about the proposed implementation.

> I think we may be solving the right problem in a more complex way than necessary.

---

## Stronger disagreement

> I would not recommend this approach in the current setup.

> I think this creates more risk than value.

> From a technical point of view, I do not think we have enough evidence to proceed with this option.

> I would prefer that we validate the assumption before making this change.

> I think we should challenge this design before we commit to it.

---

## When you need to say no

> I do not think we should move forward with this yet.

> I would be uncomfortable approving this without additional testing.

> At this stage, I cannot recommend this solution.

> We can do it, but I think we need to be clear about the trade-offs first.

> I would prefer to keep this out of production until we have validated the failure scenario.

---

# 8. French / European communication style

There is no single European style, but in many French and European workplaces, people often appreciate communication that is:

- clear;
- factual;
- not overly enthusiastic;
- respectful of expertise;
- open to debate;
- careful with commitments;
- focused on reasoning and trade-offs.

You can sound professional without using too many positive words.

## Instead of very American-style enthusiasm

Instead of:

> Amazing idea! I love it!

Use:

> Yes, that could be a good option.

Or:

> That makes sense to me.

Or:

> I think this is worth exploring.

---

Instead of:

> Absolutely, this is perfect.

Use:

> Yes, I agree with the direction.

Or:

> This looks reasonable.

Or:

> I think this is a solid approach.

---

Instead of:

> Great job, everyone!

Use:

> Thanks everyone. I think we made good progress.

Or:

> Thanks, this gives us a clear direction.

# 10. Technical review conversations

## Reviewing a design

**Developer:** We are thinking about calling the service directly from the frontend.

**Technical Advisor:**
> Technically that is possible, but I would like to check the security and dependency impact first. What is the reason for bypassing the backend in this case?

**Developer:** It would be faster to implement.

**Technical Advisor:**
> I understand. For a short-term solution, that may be attractive. My concern is that it could make authentication and future changes harder. Could we compare both options before deciding?

---

## Reviewing an API change

**Developer:** We need to add three new fields to the API.

**Technical Advisor:**
> That sounds reasonable. Are the fields optional or required?

**Developer:** Required.

**Technical Advisor:**
> In that case, we should check backward compatibility. Do we know whether existing consumers can handle the change?

**Developer:** Not yet.

**Technical Advisor:**
> Then I would suggest validating the consumers first. If needed, we could introduce the fields as optional and make them mandatory later.

---

# 11. Troubleshooting conversations

## Production incident

**Team member:** The service is returning 500 errors.

**Technical Advisor:**
> Do we know when it started?

**Team member:** Around 10:30.

**Technical Advisor:**
> Was there any deployment or configuration change around that time?

**Team member:** There was a deployment at 10:15.

**Technical Advisor:**
> Okay. I would first compare the logs before and after the deployment. We should also check whether the error affects all requests or only a specific flow.

---

## When the root cause is unclear

> At the moment, I do not think we have enough information to call this a database issue.

> Let us verify the application logs first.

> Can we reproduce the issue outside production?

> I would like to rule out configuration before we change the code.

> Let us collect the evidence first and then decide where to focus.

---

# 12. Architecture discussions

## Introducing an architecture point

> I would like to look at this from a system perspective.

> This works locally, but we should also consider how it fits into the wider architecture.

> My main question is where this responsibility should live.

> We should avoid creating unnecessary coupling between these components.

> I think ownership is important here.

> Which team will maintain this component in the long term?

---

## Discussing scalability

> The current design should work for today’s volume.

> The question is whether we expect the traffic to grow significantly.

> Do we have an estimate for peak load?

> We do not need to over-engineer this, but we should avoid a clear bottleneck.

> I would keep the design simple and monitor before adding more complexity.

---

# 13. Security and compliance discussions

> Before we proceed, I would like to confirm the data classification.

> Does this flow contain personal or sensitive data?

> Are we allowed to send this information to an external service?

> We should confirm the retention requirements.

> I would like security to review this point.

> This looks technically fine, but we should still check the compliance impact.

> We should avoid logging sensitive information.

> Can we mask this value in the logs?

---

# 14. Talking to project managers

## Explaining technical risk

> The implementation itself is not very complex, but there is some uncertainty around integration.

> The main risk is not development time; it is testing across the dependent systems.

> We can meet the date, but we may need to reduce the scope.

> I would prefer not to commit to the final date until we confirm the dependency.

> From a technical side, the timeline is possible if the interface remains stable.

---

## Asking for prioritization

> We have two technical issues, but only one is blocking the release.

> Could we confirm which one has higher business priority?

> If the deadline is fixed, we may need to choose the simpler option.

> We can do both, but not with the same level of depth in the current timeline.

---

# 15. Talking to business stakeholders

## Explaining a technical limitation

> The request is possible, but it requires a change in the way the current system is designed.

> We can support this scenario, but it will take more effort than a normal configuration change.

> The current platform does not support this directly.

> We have a workaround, but I would not recommend it as a long-term solution.

> We need to understand how important this requirement is before choosing the technical approach.

---

## Explaining trade-offs

> We can make it faster, or we can make it more flexible, but doing both will increase complexity.

> The simpler solution is easier to maintain but has fewer customization options.

> The more advanced option gives us more flexibility, but it will require more testing and support.

> There is no technical blocker. It is mainly a question of cost, time, and long-term maintenance.

---

# 16. Talking to a customer

## When you need more information

> To understand the issue better, could you share one example where the behavior is incorrect?

> Could you confirm the expected result?

> Do you know when the problem first appeared?

> Is the issue happening for all users or only some users?

> If possible, could you provide the timestamp and request ID?

---

## When you do not know yet

> I do not want to give you an incorrect answer, so I would like to verify this point first.

> We are still investigating the root cause.

> We have identified one possible cause, but we need to confirm it.

> I will review this with the team and come back with a clear recommendation.

> At this stage, I would prefer not to speculate.

---

# 17. Working with senior engineers

> I would like your view on one point.

> Do you see any architectural concern with this option?

> I have reviewed the main flow, but I would appreciate a second opinion on the failure scenario.

> My current recommendation is option A, mainly because it reduces coupling.

> I may be missing some historical context here.

> Was there a reason the system was originally designed this way?

# 18. Working with junior developers

A Technical Advisor should guide without taking over all the work.

> What have you tried so far?

> What do you think is the root cause?

> Which part of the flow are you least confident about?

> Let us go through the logic together.

> Your approach is reasonable. I would just add one check here.

> Before changing the code, how could we confirm the assumption?

> Can you think of a failure case for this design?

> Try to keep the first version simple.

> Once you have a first proposal, I am happy to review it with you.

## Positive

> This looks clear to me.

> The logic is easy to follow.

> I like that the change is small and focused.

> This is simpler than the previous version.

> The error handling looks better now.

---

## Suggestions

> Could we simplify this part?

> Do we need this additional abstraction?

> Would it be clearer to move this logic into a separate function?

> Could we add a test for the failure case?

> What happens if this value is null?

> Is there a reason we are catching the exception here?

> Could this affect existing consumers?

> I would add a short comment explaining why this behavior is needed.

---

## Concerns

> I am slightly concerned about the dependency introduced here.

> This may become difficult to maintain.

> I think we should avoid duplicating this logic.

> This could create inconsistent behavior between the two flows.

> I would prefer not to merge this until we confirm the edge case.

# 20. When someone asks for your opinion

Instead of always saying “I think”, vary your language.

> My view is that we should keep the current design.

> From a technical perspective, option B is safer.

> Based on what we know today, I would choose option A.

> My preference would be to avoid another dependency.

> The way I see it, the main issue is ownership.

> For me, the key point is maintainability.

> I would lean toward the simpler approach.

> I am comfortable with either option, but option B gives us more flexibility.

---

# 21. When you are not fully sure

> I need to verify that.

> I am not completely sure about that behavior.

> My understanding is that it works this way, but I would like to confirm.

> I believe this is configurable, but let me check.

> I do not have enough information to answer confidently yet.

> I would rather confirm before giving a final recommendation.

This style is often better than pretending to know.

# 25. Decision-making phrases

## Before the decision

> Do we have enough information to decide today?

> Is there any major concern we have not discussed?

> Are we comfortable with the risk?

> Do we need input from another team?

> Is there any blocker to choosing this option?

---

## Confirming the decision

> It sounds like we are aligned on option B.

> So the decision is to keep the existing API and update the backend logic.

> Just to confirm, we will proceed with the simpler option for the first release.

> I think we have a clear direction.

> Unless there is an objection, I suggest we proceed with this approach.

# 29. Handling ownership questions

> Which team currently owns this component?

> Who should be the long-term owner of this logic?

> I think we should clarify ownership before adding more functionality here.

> Technically we can implement it in either place, but ownership should influence the decision.

> I would prefer the logic to stay with the team that owns the business rule.

---

# 30. Handling deadlines

> What is the real deadline for this change?

> Is the date fixed, or is there some flexibility?

> If the date cannot move, we should reduce the technical scope.

> I can support the short-term solution, but we should document the follow-up work.

> I do not want the temporary solution to become the default architecture.

> We can deliver a safe first version and improve it after the release.

# 31. Handling pressure

When someone asks you to approve something quickly:

> I understand the urgency, but I would like to check one risk before approving it.

> I can give a provisional view now, but I would prefer to confirm after reviewing the logs.

> I do not want to block the team unnecessarily, but this point needs validation.

> If we proceed now, we should be clear about the risk we are accepting.

---

# 32. When a meeting has too many opinions

> I think we have several valid views.

> Maybe we should compare the options using the same criteria.

> Could we agree on the decision criteria first?

> For me, the main criteria are maintainability, delivery time, and operational risk.

> If we use those criteria, option B seems stronger.

This helps you act as an advisor rather than another person adding an opinion.

# 33. Useful decision criteria

When comparing technical solutions, you can mention:

- implementation effort;
- maintainability;
- reliability;
- security;
- performance;
- scalability;
- operational complexity;
- cost;
- dependency;
- ownership;
- supportability;
- backward compatibility;
- user impact;
- delivery time.

Example:

> If we compare the two options in terms of effort, maintainability, and operational risk, I think option A is the better fit.

# 47. Mini dialogues for common situations

## A. Developer asks for quick approval

**Developer:** Can we merge this today?

**Technical Advisor:**
> The change looks reasonable. I just want to confirm the error handling before we merge. If that is covered, I am fine to proceed.

---

## B. PM asks if the feature can be delivered

**PM:** Can we deliver this by Friday?

**Technical Advisor:**
> The core change is possible by Friday. The risk is the integration testing. If the date is fixed, I suggest we keep the first version limited to the main flow.

---

## C. Business asks for customization

**Business:** Can we make the behavior different for this customer?

**Technical Advisor:**
> Yes, technically we can. Before we do that, I would like to understand whether this is a one-off need or something we expect for other customers as well. That will affect the design.

---

## D. Team proposes a complex architecture

**Developer:** We can add a new service for this.

**Technical Advisor:**
> We can, but I am not sure we need another service for this scope. Could we first see whether the existing component can own the logic without creating a strong dependency?

---

## E. Incident with little information

**Manager:** Do we know the cause?

**Technical Advisor:**
> Not yet. We have one possible lead, but I would not call it the root cause yet. We are checking the logs and the latest deployment first.

# 48. Phrases that make your role visible

Use these naturally, not all the time.

> I would like to look at the wider technical impact.

> Let us make sure the solution fits the existing architecture.

> I want to separate what is technically possible from what is advisable.

> We should consider not only implementation but also support and maintenance.

> The question for me is whether this is the right responsibility for this component.

> I would like to understand the trade-offs before recommending an option.

> My role here is mainly to help the team choose the safest practical approach.

> I am happy to challenge the design if that helps us avoid problems later.

# 49. Things to avoid

Try not to overuse:

> Obviously...

> Clearly...

> You are wrong.

> This makes no sense.

> We always do it this way.

> That is not my problem.

> Just do it.

> Trust me.

> This is easy.

These phrases can sound dismissive, especially in international teams.

Prefer:

> My understanding is...

> I see it differently.

> Could we review the reason for this choice?

> I think there is another option.

> Let us check the impact first.

# 50. Simple formula for speaking like a Technical Advisor

A useful structure is:

**Context → Observation → Risk → Recommendation → Next step**

Example:

> We currently call the external API directly from two services.  
> I noticed that both services implement the same retry logic.  
> This may lead to inconsistent behavior and make support more difficult.  
> I would recommend moving the retry policy to a shared integration layer.  
> As a next step, we can prepare a small design proposal and review the impact.

This structure makes your communication clear and senior without using difficult vocabulary.

---

# 51. Another useful formula for meetings

**Question → Evidence → Options → Decision**

Example:

> The question is where the validation logic should live.  
> Today, the same rule is already used by three services.  
> We can either keep the logic in each service or centralize it.  
> I would recommend centralizing it because the rule is shared and likely to change.

# 53. A natural personal introduction

# 54. Final communication principles

As a Technical Advisor:

1. Ask before assuming.
2. Separate facts from opinions.
3. Explain risks without creating unnecessary fear.
4. Give options when possible.
5. Recommend, do not command.
6. Say when you need to verify something.
7. Keep technical language simple when speaking with non-technical people.
8. Explain trade-offs, not only solutions.
9. Make decisions and next steps clear.
10. Stay calm and factual when the discussion becomes difficult.

A useful sentence to remember:

> My goal is not only to find a solution that works today, but to help the team choose one that we can understand, support, and maintain tomorrow.
